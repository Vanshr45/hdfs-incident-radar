of storage blocks went wrong. Could a computer find them?

In , a Hadoop cluster of machines wrote log lines in hours. Every file it stored was cut into blocks, and every block left a trail of lines about what happened to it. I treated each trail as a diary, counted what was in it, and trained models to tell healthy diaries from broken ones. This page is what I found — including the blocks my best model still gets wrong.

By Vansh Rana · every number on this page is read from files my notebooks saved

The haystack

Hadoop splits files into blocks and keeps copies of each one on different machines. The cluster in my dataset handled blocks in hours.

Each square here is about of them.

The dataset’s authors ran hand-written rules over the log and labelled blocks as broken. That is about 1 in .

Gathered along the top, they fill of the squares.

In the real log they are scattered, and from the outside a broken block looks like any other.

The only way to tell is to read what each one went through.

1 in

blocks in the log were labelled broken. Rare enough to miss by eye, common enough that missing them matters.

Before training anything I checked which steps move with the label at all. Plain correlation on the training blocks already shows the shape of the problem: broken blocks are mostly missing things, not doing strange things.

Correlation only sees straight-line links between one count and the label. A model can combine steps, so a weak correlation here does not make an event useless.

        09

        Do broken blocks cluster?

        Not much. I fitted k-means on normal training blocks, then dropped every training block into its nearest cluster. One small cluster is mostly broken, but most broken blocks land in clusters that look normal. Clustering helped me describe the data; it would make a poor detector.

            12

            What this does not show

            • It counts steps and ignores their order. A block whose steps arrived in a strange order, but in normal numbers, looks healthy to every model here.
            • It is one cluster in . machines, hours, one Hadoop version, benchmark jobs. I have no evidence the cut-off would carry over to anything else.
            • The labels came from rules, not from failures someone confirmed. My models learned to agree with those rules.
            • Patterns, not causes. “Tried to delete a block that was not there” is the forest’s favourite clue, but it is probably a symptom of an earlier failure, not the failure itself.
            • The near-perfect scores are partly the data. Only different diaries appear in training blocks, so a model that memorises patterns does very well. Published studies report similarly high scores on this dataset.

            13

            How I did it

            Data. Loghub HDFS_v1, Zenodo record : log lines grouped into blocks. Only the event-count columns go into the models. I dropped the block id and the matrix’s own Label and Type columns, because Type is only filled in for failed blocks and would hand the answer over.

            Split. One stratified split set aside of blocks for testing (). The rest became training () and validation (). Seed everywhere. Notebook 03 ends with checks that no scaler, PCA or model ever saw a validation or test row.

            Course topics. Preprocessing, correlation, logistic regression, k-nearest neighbours, Naive Bayes, decision trees and rules, linear SVM, k-means, PCA, a small neural network, bias-variance, bagging (random forest), boosting (AdaBoost), and accuracy, precision, recall, F1, AUC, log loss and confusion matrices.

            Cut-offs. Every model’s cut-off is the one with the lowest validation cost; a block raises the alarm when its score is at or above it.
